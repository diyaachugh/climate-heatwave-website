A warning is only useful if people know what to do with it. These are the practical steps for before, during, and in the middle of a heat emergency.

Who's Most at Risk

Elderly people

Older adults regulate body temperature less efficiently and are more likely to have chronic conditions that heat can worsen.

Young children

Children's bodies heat up faster than adults' and they may not recognise or communicate early signs of heat stress.

Outdoor workers

People working in construction, agriculture or delivery face prolonged direct exposure during the hottest hours of the day.

People with chronic illness

Heart disease, diabetes, and respiratory conditions can all be aggravated by extreme heat.

Before a Heatwave

  • Drink plenty of water throughout the day, even before feeling thirsty
  • Keep ORS and essential medicines ready at home
  • Monitor weather forecasts and heat alerts regularly
  • Plan outdoor activities for the cooler morning or evening hours

During a Heatwave

  • Stay indoors whenever possible, especially during peak heat
  • Wear light-coloured, loose-fitting cotton clothing
  • Avoid direct sun exposure between 12 PM and 4 PM
  • Use fans or air conditioning, and keep spaces well ventilated
  • Check in on elderly people, children, and those with chronic illness regularly

Emergency Response

  • Call emergency services immediately if someone shows signs of heat stroke — confusion, hot dry skin, or loss of consciousness
  • Move the affected person to a cool, shaded place right away
  • Offer cool water if they are conscious and able to drink
  • Loosen or remove excess clothing and fan them while waiting for help
Remember: heat stroke is a medical emergency. If someone stops sweating, becomes confused, or loses consciousness in extreme heat, treat it the same as any other emergency — act immediately and call for help.
